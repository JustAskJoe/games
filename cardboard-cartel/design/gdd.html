Cardboard Cartel — Game Design Document

Overview

Cardboard Cartel is a collector sim with an idle backbone, set in a world where a goofy villain syndicate stole every trading card ever printed and is now dripping them back onto the market. Every card that will ever exist is in one fixed pot from day one; players race NPC rivals (and later each other) for each release.

Pitch: "What if the whole TCG market restarted, with every card sealed in one vault, released a little at a time?"

Pillars

  1. The pot is real. Every set has a fixed print run. Drops release slices; once a set is out, it's out forever.

  2. Every pack is a ritual. Ripping is tactile, and big hits always get a manual Slow Peel.

  3. Collect your way. Four lanes (Collector, Reseller, Grader, Shopkeeper). Start in one, grow into any.

  4. A living hobby. NPC rivals, Cartel grunts and a rumor mill make a solo world feel crowded.

World and tone

The Cartel is the reason the market exists the way it does, and it's played for laughs: villains who monologue, overreach and blast off.

  • The Cartel. Years ago it hoarded, bought out or swiped every card in the world. Now it drips its hoard back out to fund its schemes. Its vault holds everything: sealed cases, kids' stolen binders, graded slabs, shoeboxes. That's why condition varies so wildly.

  • The Attic Stash. The one binder the Cartel missed: yours. Every player starts by opening it.

  • Cartel grunts. Recurring named goons show up at drops, cut lines, buy out shelves and sell suspiciously "fresh" resealed packs.

  • Rivals. 100 NPC collectors on a Top 100 leaderboard, each with a lane, a budget and an aggression stat. A few are named recurring characters with dialogue (Pokémon-rival energy); the rest live in the ticker. Launch uses generic names and silhouettes; portraits come in the art pass.

  • One flagship TCG. All 20+ sets are the history of a single fictional card game, with original monsters and art. No real IP, names or numbers. The grail of the first set is Emberwyrm, which needs its own silhouette, not an orange dragon with a flame tail.

  • CGA (Card Grading Authority) is the parody grader, with its own slab and label design.

  • TCG only. No sports cards or retro games.

The Pot

The pot is the whole game: 20+ sets and 2,000+ cards exist from day one, each set with a fixed print run, released in slices by the Cartel and never reprinted.

How it works

  • Fixed print runs. Every set has a total in the pot, for example 5,000 Founders boxes. A drop releases a slice ("100 Founders boxes this week"); the rest stays locked. When the last slice is out, no new product of that set is ever made.

  • Release rhythm. One big Release Day a week plus small daily restocks. Each wave is a Cartel-chosen mix of eras: mostly modern, some oddball promos, occasionally a sliver of vintage. Modern is always the easiest to get.

  • How you get drop product (mixed by tier):

    • Modern: first-come retail rush.

    • Mid-tier: raffles.

    • Vintage: blind auctions against rivals.

  • Rumor mill. Upcoming drops leak through the ticker as hints and rumors, not a calendar. Better intel comes from Rep and lane skills.

  • Recirculation, not reprints. Released supply sits with rivals and NPCs. When a rival retires, their collection hits the market: an "estate find" made of existing supply, so the pool stays truly fixed.

  • Sinks shrink it. Shredding, Master Binder locks and damage permanently remove cards, so out-of-print sets get rarer over time.

  • Vintage boxes. A typical solo career sees 3–5 vintage grail-set boxes. Each one forces the core choice: rip it, or hold it sealed while world supply shrinks. Loose vintage packs (estates, Cartel pallets, rival lots) are the regular dose between.

  • Population caps. Each card's perfect-grade population = print run × that era's gem rate (Founders 0.4%, Burger Baron 0.1%, Modern 8%). Rivals claim slots on a schedule, so the race for the last CGA 10 is real.

Core loop

The game is balanced, with a calendar heartbeat: active when the market moves on Release Day, idle while the shop runs between drops.

The rumor mill feeds Release Day; everything you pull crosses the sorting desk and ends up shredded, graded, sold or kept, and the proceeds fund the next drop.

  • Ripping. Every pack you open is tactile. Staff may auto-rip cheap modern packs, but any hit above your value threshold drops face-down into the Pulls Box for a manual Slow Peel. Vintage and premium product is always opened by hand.

  • Staff (idle). Sorter sleeves and shreds; Lister sells commons and fills small bounties; Scout brings back garage-sale and estate leads (leads, not purchases). Staff never grade, never rip vintage, never sell above your threshold.

  • Caps. The unsorted tray holds about 8 hours of output, supplies run out, and unsleeved cards decay. A week away means a full tray, not a fortune.

  • Morning Open (3–5 minutes on return): peel the Pulls Box, collect grading returns, read the rival recap, check the Cartel's move, pick which leads to chase.

Lanes

Four lanes replace the old archetypes: your backstory picks a starting lane, and the skill tree lets you branch into all four. Ripping isn't a lane, because it's everyone's core ritual.

  • The Grader is a specialist who submits to CGA, not someone who runs their own grading company.

  • Rivals use the same four lanes, which makes their behavior learnable: you bid against a Collector differently than a Reseller.

Economy

Prices run at real-world scale ($0.10 commons up to six-figure grails) and are driven by five factors, so the market moves without any external APIs.

\text{price} = \text{rarity base} \times \text{condition} \times \text{grade} \times \text{scarcity} \times \text{trend}
FactorFirst-pass rule
GradeVintage: CGA 10 about 10× raw NM, CGA 9 about 2.5×, CGA 8 about 1×. Modern: CGA 10 about 2–3×. Grading is a vintage play, not something you do to everything
Sealed scarcityMSRP × (starting supply ÷ remaining supply)^0.6, so prices climb without going vertical
Trend±25% weekly swing per category, from Cartel moves, rival buying and the meta
MetaWeekly NPC tournaments make certain cards "meta" and spike them. Playable and collectible value diverge, as in the real hobby

Condition is skill-gated. Subgrades (centering, corners, edges, surface) start hidden; Rep and the Grader lane unlock better reads. Unsleeved raw cards slowly decay.

Selling works both ways: quick-sell to NPC offers for less, or list your own price and wait longer for more.

Cartel moves run on a weekly calendar:

  • Flood: dumps a subset; its price drops about 30% for 48 hours, then recovers to about 80% over the week.

  • Buy-back bounty: pays up to 140% for specific cards the Cartel is missing.

  • Pallet drop: a blind auction against rivals.

  • Heat: beating the Cartel builds heat; at max it locks you out of its direct drops for a few days. Garage sales and estates stay open.

Rival bids follow bid = market value × lane multiplier ± small noise. Collectors pay about 1.2× for pristine vintage and 0.6× for modern; Resellers never go above about 0.85×; Shopkeepers overpay for sealed; Graders chase raw cards with high hidden grades.

Risks: market crashes stay in (they come from floods and meta shifts). Resealed packs exist as a light Cartel-grunt gag that Grader and Reseller skills learn to spot. Scams and shipping damage are cut; counterfeits are parked for post-launch.

Going broke is soft: hit zero and you can sell to the Cartel at a loss or take a side job. Never a game over.

Sinks: the Bulk Shredder (bulk becomes cardstock for sleeves and holders), Master Binder set-locks (cards leave circulation for permanent bonuses) and transit damage on budget grading.

Progression and goals

A solo career runs about 30–40 hours to the Cartel finale, with Rep opening doors and cash deciding what you can afford once inside.

  • Gating: Rep + cash. Ten Rep tiers each unlock new sources, staff slots, VIP shows and drop access. Income grows roughly linearly within a tier, so numbers stay realistic.

  • Long-term goals: set completion (binders, master sets, variants); Pop 1 and perfect grades; net worth and the Top 100 rival ladder; vault and museum rating.

  • Celebrating pulls: big hits go to the in-game feed, where rivals and grunts react. No export pipeline at launch.

  • Endgame: Cartel arcs. The solo career has a story arc ending in a goofy finale where you foil the Cartel's first scheme; free play continues after. Each new career or online season brings a new Cartel scheme with its own wave mix and rules.

  • Prestige: Franchise Expansion. When you max out a location, it becomes a permanent museum earning a trickle of income, and you open a new one with your permanent bonuses. Your collection is never wiped.

Presentation

The game is a 2D top-down desk built in Unity as flat card planes on a 3D desk, so the same scene works on a monitor and in XR.

  • Flat (launch): an orthographic top-down camera over your workspace. Cards slide, fan and flip; it plays like a 2D game.

  • XR (port): the desk becomes a real tabletop. Rip packs with your hands, hold a card up to the light to read its surface, slide slabs into a vault on your wall. Target Pico, Quest and SteamVR through OpenXR.

  • Card art: AI-assisted for one consistent style across 2,000+ cards. Variants reuse base illustrations, and art is produced ahead of each set's release, not all before launch.

Monetization

Pay once (~$15–20) and you get the whole pot. Drops are never sold.

  • Never sell access to drops or packs. Paid drops would make it a loot box and make the scarcity feel rigged.

  • Optional cosmetics: slab skins, desk themes, binder covers, XR room themes.

  • Online seasons are free. Each is a fresh pot drip that brings players back: marketing, not a revenue line.

  • No expansion DLC. Every card is in the pot from day one.

Scope and roadmap

Ship the solo career first, built so online seasons and XR bolt on later; both are post-launch.

Nothing past pre-production starts until the rip feels great and the spreadsheet economy holds up over a simulated career.

Cut or parked: a playable card battle mode (simulated meta instead; Game Nights maybe later), sports cards and retro games, counterfeits, scams and shipping damage, exportable clips, a player-run grading company.
