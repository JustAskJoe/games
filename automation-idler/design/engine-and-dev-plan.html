Automation Idler: Engine & Dev Plan

The approach

Build a flat web game on top of a separate automation engine, use your own automations as the first content, and keep every choice spatial-ready so the XR version is a new client, not a rebuild.

  • Engine first, town second. The engine runs real automations and records what they did. The town only reads that record and draws it. Every client (web, phone, Steam, PICO) talks to the same engine.

  • Real value is the core currency. Players earn Hours when an automation actually runs and saves them time. Idle mechanics fill the gaps between runs, but the town grows because their life got easier.

  • Your automations are the vertical slice. Reminders, the weekly H-E-B order, the morning email and the studio numbers report are already built. Each becomes a starter building, so there is nothing to invent for v1.

  • Operators are scripted by default. Characters run on written dialogue and templates. A model call only happens when an automation needs one or a player chooses to talk, and every call is metered.

  • Spatial-ready from day one. One React codebase, an engine API any client can call, and an art pipeline that can become a 3D tabletop town later (see Open decisions).

Engine data model

Every building wraps exactly one real automation, every run of that automation pays Hours into the town, and the town only grows from what the engine records.

A player has one town. A town holds many buildings. Each building wraps one automation and is staffed by one operator. Automations use integrations and produce runs, and each successful run pays Hours.

How the loop works

  1. An automation runs on its trigger, and the engine logs a run.

  2. A successful run pays its minutes saved into the town as Hours, and the building plays its working animation.

  3. A failed run turns the building to its broken state, and the operator flags it, so a broken automation is something you can see and fix in the town.

  4. Hours buy upgrades, decorations and new plots. Town tier rises with active automations and connected integrations, which is your first, small, medium, large, HUGE growth story.

  5. AI cost is tracked on every run against the player's monthly budget, which gives you cost per user straight from the data.

Development phases

Five phases get you to a public beta in roughly four to five months at side-project pace, and spatial starts only once the flat game takes off.

Each phase ends at a gate, and nothing moves to the next phase until it is met. The estimates assume nights and weekends with agents doing most of the coding; Phase 1 will show the real pace.

Who leads each phase

  • 0 · Foundations: you, the tech lead and the lawyer. The Engine Engineer drafts the API spec.

  • 1 · Engine MVP: the Engine Engineer builds, the QA Tester runs automations against sandbox accounts.

  • 2 · Town prototype: the Client Engineer, Game Writer and Art Assistant. The artist starts on the style guide.

  • 3 · Friends alpha: every agent, plus the security reviewer and your first testers.

  • 4 · Public beta: the Economy Designer tunes balance, the artist finishes the art pass, community and marketing come on.

  • 5 · Spatial: the Client Engineer adds the WebSpatial client, tested in the PICO emulator through pico-cli. The employment IP check decides when this can start.

How to move fast

Speed comes from cutting scope and buying infrastructure, not from building faster.

  • Be the first player. Run your four automations through the engine and check your own town every day from Phase 1. You will find what is fun and what is broken before anyone else does.

  • One building end to end before the second. Get the Lanterns working from trigger to run to Hours to animation, then copy the pattern.

  • Buy, don't build. Hosted auth and database, a hosted job scheduler, an off-the-shelf renderer and placeholder asset packs. None of that is the game.

  • Templates before a builder. In v1, players pick an automation from a list and fill in a few settings. A full drag-and-drop automation builder comes much later, if ever.

  • A playable build every Friday. Even when it is ugly. The friends you are lining up for the PC Remote beta are a natural first tester group from Phase 3.

  • A "later" list. Anything outside the current phase's exit criteria goes there, not into the build.

  • Agents work in parallel. Engine and client agents can build at the same time once the engine API is written down in Phase 0.

Hosting and account tokens, in plain terms

Rent the hard parts: a managed integration platform holds players' account keys, and a hosted backend runs the engine, so you never build login plumbing yourself.

What a token is. When a player clicks "Connect Gmail," Google hands your app a token, a long-lived key that lets the engine act on that account. Whoever stores those keys is responsible if they leak, so this is the most security-sensitive part of the game.

What the engine needs to run.

  • A backend that runs on a schedule even when nobody has the game open. A hosted service (database, player accounts and scheduled jobs in one) covers this.

  • An integration layer that does the "Connect your account" flow, stores the tokens and calls each app. Managed platforms such as Composio, Nango and Pipedream Connect do exactly this: players connect their own accounts and your code acts on their behalf, with the platform managing the logins and keys.

  • An AI gateway in the engine that meters every model call against the player's budget.

Why a managed platform fits. These platforms come with large catalogs of prebuilt integrations, Pipedream lists over 3,000 APIs, so 25 integrations becomes picking from a menu instead of building 25 login flows. Composio is fully managed and SOC 2 Type II compliant, which helps with your security review. The tradeoff is a monthly bill that grows with players, which goes into cost per user.

The Gmail catch. Reading someone's inbox uses Google's restricted scopes, which require verification plus a third-party security assessment, repeated every year. Send-only access (gmail.send) is classed as sensitive, not restricted, and needs no assessment. So v1 sends mail only, which is all the morning email needs, and inbox reading waits until the game earns it.

Recommendation for Phase 0: your partner picks the hosted backend, and the two of you trial one integration platform by connecting Google Calendar and Gmail send. Whichever feels easiest wins.
