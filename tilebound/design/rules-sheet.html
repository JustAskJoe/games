Tilebound uses one four-seat ruleset: American-style mahjong with original tiles and an original House Card. Every table in the game runs on it. Master matches, Mahj Night and the tutorial lessons only change its settings (seats, hand size, which rules are on).

This sheet is what the prototype engine implements. Numbers marked as targets are guesses that the bot simulation and the first playtests will tune. All hands on the card are original; never copy the National Mah Jongg League card.

A hand on the card is always 14 tiles. Some hands name a specific suit; most say "any one suit" or "three different suits", so any suit can fill them.

Setup

Four seats: East, South, West, North. East is the dealer for the hand; the dealer seat moves one place to the right after every hand.

  1. Shuffle and build the wall. 152 tiles make four sides of 38 (19 stacks of 2), one side in front of each seat.

  2. Deal. Every seat gets 13 tiles; East gets a 14th and discards first.

  3. Blossoms stay in the hand. Unlike Chinese mahjong, Blossoms are not set aside and replaced; they are part of hands on the card.

  4. Play goes to the right (counterclockwise), as in American mahjong.

That leaves 99 tiles to draw, so a hand can run at most about 25 rounds before the wall runs out.

The Swap

Before the first discard, everyone trades tiles they don't want; this is American mahjong's Charleston. It lets every player steer toward a hand on the card.

  1. First Swap (always). Pass 3 tiles to the right, then 3 across, then 3 to the left. On the last pass you may pass blind: pass on 1–3 of the tiles you just received without looking.

  2. Second Swap (optional). If all four seats agree, pass 3 left, 3 across, 3 right. One "no" skips it. Bots vote yes when their hand is 4 or more tiles from Ready.

  3. Courtesy pass (optional). You and the player across trade 0–3 tiles; both pass the smaller number either of you offers.

Mimics can never be passed. The prototype (v2) includes the first and second Swap and the courtesy pass. In the bot simulation, the second Swap cut wall games from about 45% to about 25%.

A turn

On your turn you draw one tile from the wall, then discard one tile face up into the pond, naming it. Your hand is always 13 tiles between turns.

Calling a discard. When someone discards, any other player may claim it:

  • To expose a group. You may call a discard to complete a Set of 3, 4 or 5 (a pung, kong or quint) and lay that group face up. You can only do this for a hand on the card marked X (exposed), and only if the group matches that hand.

  • To win. Any discard can be called to declare Mahj, even to finish a pair or a single.

  • No calls for pairs or singles except to win.

  • Who gets it. A call to win beats a call to expose; between equal calls, the player next in turn order wins.

  • After a call, the caller discards, and play continues to the caller's right. Players between the discarder and the caller lose their turn.

Mimics.

  • A Mimic can stand in for any tile in a group of 3, 4 or 5, in your hand or in an exposed group.

  • A Mimic can never be part of a pair or a single.

  • Swapping a Mimic out. On your turn, if any exposed group (yours or another player's) contains a Mimic and you hold the real tile it stands for, you may swap them and take the Mimic.

  • A discarded Mimic is dead: nobody can call it.

Dead hands. If your exposed groups no longer fit any hand on the card, your hand is dead: you keep drawing and discarding but cannot win. In beginner mode the game blocks calls that would kill your hand, so this only happens in Club rules mode.

Winning and scoring

When your 14 tiles match a hand on the card, say Mahj! and lay them down. The hand ends at once.

How you wonWho pays you
On someone's discardThe discarder pays 2× the hand's points; the other two pay 1×
Drawn from the wall yourselfAll three pay 2×
No Mimics in your hand ("clean")Double the payment above (not for hands that never allow Mimics)

If the wall runs out and nobody has won, it's a wall game: nobody pays and the next hand starts. Target: under 25% of hands end as wall games.

Scores are a running total that starts at 0, so they can go negative. A Mahj Night evening is 4 hands; the highest total wins the evening.

Master matches

A master match is a four-hand match at the master's own table: you, the master and two of the master's regulars. You win by finishing the four hands with a higher total than the master. You don't need the highest total at the table, and you don't need to win every hand.

  1. Earn your seat. Beat 2–3 regulars in single hands at the parlor's casual tables. They play the parlor's card, so you learn the master's hands before you face them. Each win gives a hint, such as "Mags never passes Ember in the Swap".

  2. Favored tiles. Before the match you pick 6 tile types from your collection. The engine makes sure those 6 sit in your side of the wall, by trading places with the same tiles from other sides. The 152-tile set never changes; only where tiles sit does.

  3. The parlor's card. The starter card plus 1–2 parlor hands, worth a bit more.

  4. The house rule. One rule twist per parlor.

  5. Ties. If you and the master finish level, play one more hand.

Helpers and plain words

Every helper runs on one number: tiles from Ready, the fewest tiles your hand needs to change to complete any hand on the card. Mahjong players call it shanten.

  • Ready counter: always on screen, for example "3 tiles from Ready".

  • Target hands: the two closest hands on the card are highlighted; tiles that fit them glow.

  • Hint button: suggests a discard that keeps you closest to Ready.

  • Call prompt: pops up only when a discard actually helps you, with "Take it" or "Pass".

  • Beginner guard: blocks calls that would make your hand dead.

Every helper can be switched off. Club rules mode turns them all off and switches the screen to table words.

Open questions
