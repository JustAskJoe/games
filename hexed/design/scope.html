Hexed — Game Scope

The pitch

Hexed is a cozy witch-shop match-3 with a slow-burn romance, told through the tarot. You inherit a run-down apothecary in a cursed seaside town, earn ingredients on the board, brew remedies for the townsfolk, and fall for one of three people who are each tied up in the curse.

It is a candidate for the One Fifty Labs shortlist. A 10-level playable prototype already exists (Hexed v1), so the puzzle half is proven; this scope covers the hook that makes people stay.

What no other match-3 does:

  • The tarot spread is a mechanic and a story beat. The same three cards that bend the board also steer the next scene.

  • The Lovers card is the route choice. Chapter VI is where you pick who you pursue.

  • The Tower is the midpoint crisis. The romance breaks exactly where the Major Arcana says it should.

Audience and tone

The player is a woman aged roughly 18 to 40 who reads romantasy, follows WitchTok or tarot accounts, and plays a puzzle game on her phone every night. She will play for the story and stay for the shop, and she buys cosmetics when the art is beautiful.

Tone is cozy with heat underneath: warm candlelight, flirting, longing glances, a slow burn that pays off in the last chapters. Suggestive, never explicit.

Rating target: 12+ on the App Store, Teen on Google Play (IARC 12). That keeps full ad reach and avoids age gates.

Core loop

Every level won pays twice: stars move the story, ingredients feed the shop. Neither system is a dead-end currency.

Brewing is what keeps the loop turning: charms brewed from matched ingredients become the boosters that get players through harder chapters, so the shop and the board need each other.

Structure: Book One is the Major Arcana

Book One is 22 chapters, one per Major Arcana card, at 14 levels each: 308 levels. That lands on your 300 almost exactly, and it gives the story a shape every tarot fan already knows, the Fool's Journey from 0 to XXI.

How levels and story connect (the model the big narrative match-3s use):

  • Each level won earns a star. Stars are the story currency, not the levels themselves.

  • Each chapter is a list of about 14 tasks in your shop and town: "Fix the brewing table", "Read for the baker", "Meet Corvin at the pier". Each costs 1 or 2 stars.

  • Doing a task plays a short scene (a few lines of dialogue, sometimes an illustration) and changes something you can see in the shop.

  • So yes, it is "pages of the story", but the player chooses which page to turn next. That choice is what makes it feel like her story.

Pacing at about 3 minutes a level and 10 to 15 levels a day: Book One is roughly 15 hours of play and 3 to 5 weeks for a daily player. Live content has to be ready before the first players finish.

Each chapter opens by teaching its card's twist in 2 or 3 easy levels, mixes it with earlier twists in the middle, and ends with one hard "card level" before the chapter's big scene. The prototype covers 0 to IX; these are the 12 twists still to design:

Rules that keep the slow burn honest:

  • Shared spine, separate hearts. About 70% of scenes are the shared curse plot; about 30% are route scenes. Each route gets its own version of the key beats below.

  • Nobody kisses before XIV. Chapters 0 to XIII are glances, banter, near misses and one interrupted moment. Restraint is the product.

  • The Tower breaks it. At XVI the curse's source points at your love interest (or their family). The relationship cracks, and the player has to earn it back through XVII to XIX.

  • The World pays it off. XXI ends with the curse broken, a big kiss, a fade to black, and an epilogue the next morning. Suggestive, warm, rated 12+.

  • Route switching is allowed until X, then locked for Book One. Finishing Book One unlocks a "read another heart" replay of the route scenes.

The beats sit on cards whose meanings fit them, so tarot fans feel the story was built around the deck rather than painted on.

Three things to do in the shop:

  1. Decorate. Most tasks offer three style choices (cottage, gothic, celestial). Choices are permanent and shown off in screenshots. This is the share-on-social moment.

  2. Serve customers. About 30 recurring townsfolk bring small problems ("my bread won't rise", "I need courage for an interview"). You read for them, then brew the fix from ingredients you matched. Each customer has a 3 to 5 visit mini-story.

  3. Brew. Remedies sell for coins; charms become boosters for the board (a Candle to start, +3 moves, a shuffle). This replaces buying hammers with gems.

Tarot systems

The tarot shows up in four places, and the deck the player collects ties them together.

  • Level spreads. Past, Present, Future before each card level (already in the prototype). Spreads become random from the player's own deck after chapter V.

  • Reversed cards. An opt-in harder version of any spread. Pays double stars and unlocks a cosmetic card back. This is the difficulty dial for skilled players.

  • Story readings. Before a big scene you read for yourself; the cards drawn change the scene's flavor (a reversed Lovers before a date means it goes awkwardly, a Star means it goes sweetly). Different dialogue, same plot outcome, so it stays cheap to write.

  • Daily card. One card a day with a short, accurate meaning and a small reward. A gentle ritual, not a nag.

The collectible deck. The player collects all 78 cards over time. Each card has standard art plus premium art variants (illustrated by different artists). Collecting the full deck is the long-term goal after the story, and the art variants are a natural thing to sell.

Roadmap

Next step is Prototype v2: a shop screen, stars that buy story tasks, and the first meeting with one love interest, built on the existing prototype. That is the cheapest test of whether the hook works.

Only the first phase is something you and I can build alone. From the vertical slice on, the writing and art need real people or a publisher, which is the main thing to weigh against the other shortlist games.

Risks and open decisions

The biggest risk is the writing and art bill, not the code. A romance that reads flat sinks the whole hook, so the first spend should prove the writing.

Open decisions for you:

  • Keep the name Hexed, or pick something that sounds more like romance?

  • Three love interests, or two for launch?

  • Is the player's witch fully customizable, or a fixed heroine with outfit choices? Fixed is far cheaper for illustrations.

  • Commission a writer and artist, or pitch to a publisher first?
